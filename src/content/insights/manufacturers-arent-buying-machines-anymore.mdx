After years of auditing manufacturers, I've noticed that equipment rarely causes the biggest problems. The hand-offs between people, processes and systems usually do. A machine can run exactly as specified and a business can still be losing money at the point where its output meets the next step, and nobody owns that gap.

So I'm not convinced every manufacturer needs more automation. Many need better visibility into where their real constraints are before buying anything - and that's not a conversation a trade show floor is built to have with you.

## What foodpro 2026 actually showed

Walking the floor at foodpro in Melbourne this year, it would have been easy to assume the story was all robotics and AI. Processing lines, packaging automation, smart sensors and AI-assisted compliance tools were everywhere, and the show's own programming leaned hard into what's broadly called Industry 4.0 - a catch-all term for connecting machines, data and software so a factory can see and respond to what's happening in closer to real time.

But the more revealing conversations, consistently, weren't about the machines themselves. They were about what happens in the months after the machine arrives. Suppliers fielded detailed questions about local servicing, spare parts availability and who actually answers the phone at 2am when a line goes down. Buyers weren't just comparing specifications between competing systems - they were comparing risk.

That's a meaningful shift from a purely feature-driven purchase mindset, and it lines up with what the wider data shows: manufacturers have learned, often expensively, that the sticker price of a machine is a small part of its real cost. Downtime during implementation, training time, and the cost of a repair that has to wait on a part from overseas can outweigh an attractive upfront discount many times over. For anything touching critical production, after-sales support isn't a nice-to-have - it belongs in the same risk conversation as safety and compliance.

## Why "digital transformation" so often disappoints

If automation has an ROI problem, digital transformation - the broader push to connect your production data, orders, inventory and quality records into one coherent picture - has an expectations problem.

Manufacturing SMEs consistently report the same barriers to getting real value from digital tools: limited in-house digital expertise, tight budgets, and simply not having the time to run a project properly alongside day-to-day operations. Larger manufacturers can absorb a rocky software rollout. A twenty-person operation usually can't.

The instinct this creates is understandable but often wrong: wait until you can afford "the proper system" and do it all at once. In practice, the manufacturers getting genuine value tend to do the opposite. They start by identifying which system should own which piece of information - orders inform production planning, production activity updates inventory, quality issues get flagged quickly - and fix the two or three worst gaps first. A full platform replacement is sometimes the right answer eventually. It's rarely the right starting point.

## A tale of two packing lines

Picture two mid-sized food manufacturers, both frustrated by the same packing line, both with the budget to fix it. Call them Business A and Business B.

Business A sees a faster automated packer at foodpro, gets a competitive quote, and signs off within a fortnight. It's a reasonable-looking machine from a reputable supplier. Six months later, output on the line has barely moved. The new packer is fast, but it's starved of product because the upstream mixing step, never reviewed, still runs at the same pace it always did. The bottleneck didn't disappear. It just moved twenty metres down the line, and now there's a six-figure asset sitting partly idle waiting for its turn.

Business B has the same problem and the same budget, but spends three weeks first mapping where time is actually lost across the whole line, using downtime logs and quality records that had never really been looked at together before. It turns out packing wasn't the real constraint at all: changeovers between product runs were. Business B ends up spending a smaller amount on a changeover process improvement and staff training, and holds the packer purchase for a later stage once the rest of the line can actually feed it. Output rises within the quarter, without the big capital outlay.

Same industry, same starting frustration, same available budget. The difference wasn't the machine on offer. It was whether the buyer understood their own process well enough to know what they were actually solving before they picked up the phone.

## Quality data is the cheapest technology you already own

Here's the part that gets overlooked most often, and it's the one small and mid-sized manufacturers are best placed to use: you likely already have the information you need to make a good investment decision. It's just not being looked at as decision-making data.

The concept of "cost of poor quality" - all the money lost to scrap, rework, returns and the extra checking that happens because a process isn't reliable - has been studied for decades, and [the American Society for Quality has long put the figure at somewhere between 15% and 20% of revenue](https://asq.org/quality-resources/cost-of-quality) for businesses that haven't actively worked to reduce it. World-class operations, by contrast, run closer to 3-5%. That gap isn't closed by buying a faster machine. It's closed by knowing, specifically, where and why things go wrong in the first place.

A functioning quality management system is what turns that vague, expensive gap into something you can act on. If you're already tracking nonconformances, internal audit findings and recurring rework, you have a far stronger business case than "the team says the packing line feels slow." You have "this specific step accounts for most of our downtime, here's what it's costing us, and here's what a fix needs to achieve to be worth it." That's the difference between buying a machine and buying a solution to a problem you can actually measure - and it's a far easier case to take to a bank, an investor or your own board.

## A simple framework for deciding what to invest in next

None of this means manufacturers should stop investing. Ai Group's own research shows firms that keep spending on digital tools and automation through a downturn tend to come out the other side more competitive, not less. The point isn't to spend less. It's to spend on the right thing, in the right order.

A practical starting sequence looks like this:

- **Name the constraint, not the solution.** What is actually costing you the most right now - rework, a bottleneck, an unfillable role, a compliance gap? Start there, not with a product category.
- **Get a baseline before you get a quote.** Understand your current process flow, volume, downtime and quality data well enough to describe the problem in numbers. If you can't yet, that's the first thing worth fixing.
- **Price the whole investment, not just the machine.** Installation, training, servicing, spare parts and expected downtime during implementation all belong in the same decision as the purchase price.
- **Check the support, not just the specification.** Ask what happens after the sale as seriously as you ask what the equipment does on day one.
- **Stage it.** Fix the two or three worst gaps first, in machinery, systems or process, rather than trying to modernise everything in one project.

## Key takeaways

- The best operators are starting equipment conversations with the business problem, not the machine - the hand-offs between people, processes and systems usually cause more damage than the equipment itself.
- A meaningful share of automation and digital projects underdeliver, not because the technology fails, but because the problem was never clearly defined or measured beforehand.
- The purchase price of equipment is often the smallest part of its real cost - training, servicing, spare parts and downtime usually matter more.
- You likely already have the data you need to make a better decision, in the form of quality, rework and downtime records that already exist somewhere in your business.
- Staging your investment around your single biggest constraint beats trying to modernise the whole operation at once.

## Where to start

Australian manufacturing doesn't have a shortage of good technology to buy. It has a shortage of businesses starting from a clear, evidence-based view of what they actually need before a supplier walks in the door. That view doesn't require a big project or a consultant on-site for months - it usually starts with an honest look at where your current process, quality and safety controls actually stand.
